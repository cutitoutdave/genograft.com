## Research themes

<div class="gs-card-grid" markdown="0">

<div class="gs-card">
  <span class="gs-card-icon">🦎</span>
  <h3>Mammalian complex tissue regeneration</h3>
  <p>Mice can regenerate the tip of an amputated digit — the best-studied example of mammalian complex tissue regeneration. We use mouse digit regeneration to identify the conserved genes and regulatory sequences that drive blastema formation, patterning, and outgrowth. Why regeneration succeeds in the distal digit, and fails just millimeters proximal, is the question we keep returning to.</p>
</div>

<div class="gs-card">
  <span class="gs-card-icon">🧬</span>
  <h3>Gene therapy constructs for proximal limb regeneration</h3>
  <p>Building on the regeneration biology above, we design and test gene-therapy constructs aimed at extending regenerative competence into the proximal digit and limb — tissues that today heal with scar instead of regeneration. This is where genoplastic surgery becomes a real surgical tool.</p>
</div>

<div class="gs-card">
  <span class="gs-card-icon">🤰</span>
  <h3>Fetal scarless tissue repair</h3>
  <p>Mid-gestational fetal skin heals without a scar. Adult skin almost never does. We study the molecular programs that distinguish scarless fetal repair from adult fibrotic repair, with the goal of reactivating fetal-like healing in the adult wound.</p>
</div>

<div class="gs-card">
  <span class="gs-card-icon">🧭</span>
  <h3>Evolutionary lessons from regenerating species</h3>
  <p>Salamanders, zebrafish, and certain mammals regenerate complex tissues that humans cannot. We mine evolutionarily conserved factors — and the regulatory elements that switch them on — for clues that can be brought back to human limb amputation and wound repair.</p>
</div>

</div>

## Translational mission

We work at the bench, but we keep our eyes on the operating room. Every project is evaluated against a single question: _could this become a therapy a surgeon hands their patient?_ Our pipeline runs from molecular biology and mouse genetics through gene-delivery vector design and toward early translational studies.

Support for this work comes from the **National Institutes of Health** and prior funding from the **Duke Office of Physician-Scientist Development**.
